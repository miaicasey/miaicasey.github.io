Patient App

Every patient gets a sourced, plain-language record of every consult, in one place that's theirs to keep.

Context

Heidi writes a clinical-grade record of every consult and leaves it inside the clinician’s workflow. Patients forget 40 to 80% of a consult on the way to the car park, around 10% of clinicians read material a patient sends ahead, and the handoff that would fix both (print, scan, email by hand) happens for a small minority of consults. Heidi had no patient surface, and any surface that advised in its own voice would carry medical device obligations in the UK and EU.

Problem

Build Heidi’s first patient-facing product so that it is useful on day one, grows through the clinician the patient already trusts, and stays outside device classification by design.

Goals and measures

A patient receives their consult documents, can ask about them and get answers cited back to the clinician’s words, and keeps everything in one place. Measures: clinician send rate, first-week retention of senders against non-senders, patient open and return rate, and zero advisory behaviour in the chat.

Phase 1: Discovery and the bet (June)

Set the direction and the constraints before anything was built.

  • Synthesised the first round of patient and clinician research into four findings: clinician control unlocks adoption, the post-consult gap is the patient’s biggest pain, privacy is the top trust blocker, roll out by specialty with psychiatry last.
  • Set the regulatory posture with the Regulatory Affairs Lead: retrieval from a patient’s own documents stays outside device classification in AU and the US; UK and EU gated behind registration.
  • Agreed the security baseline with the CEO and the security lead: year of birth plus clinician name to open a shared document.
  • Named the product, set a closed alpha as the pilot model and wrote the first roadmap.

Phase 2: M0, Send to Patient (July to October)

Shipped the smallest version of the loop and read it.

  • Scoped the first experiment: a clinician emails a password-protected patient document from inside Heidi, every document type minus a clinical blocklist.
  • Ran design QA with the two Principal Product Designers and the privacy pass with legal; wrote the events spec and the staged rollout plan the CEO signed off.
  • Launched to 10% of free users, held the ramp while tracking was broken rather than read a blind cohort, re-released once events fired and ramped to 100%.
  • Read the result: senders retain about 25% better in week one; under 1% of exposed clinicians sent. Wrote the review and seven hypotheses.

Phase 3: M0.1 Correspondence, the second solution (October)

Reframed the problem from a button to a workflow.

  • Found three separate send paths in the product, with M0 covering one of them; clinicians still left Heidi to send most documents.
  • Grounded the reframe in evidence from a vet, a secondary care secretary workflow and enterprise buyers paying for letter sending elsewhere.
  • Wrote the Correspondence brief: one send flow for any document to a clinician, a patient, or both, built on Letter Flow’s delivery, with the two older CTAs retired into it.
  • Recorded six options considered and rejected so anyone revisiting the decision has its reasoning intact.

Phase 4: M1, the app (July to November)

Took the app from flows to a build engineering could start. I brought design, legal, security, mobile, web and engineering onto one agreed architecture across pods, so each team could build its part without duplicating the patient work. Mobile was involved early to connect implementation to the designs, and the web team had the landing-page brief ready for the product’s front door.

  • Mapped every journey (access, account, re-entry, upload, documents, chat, share) and wrote the ten design tickets the covering Principal Product Designer closed before build.
  • Drove the data model principle that patients are their own entity, and ran the architecture jam that locked many-to-many profiles, a separate patient service and one-way snapshot publishing.
  • Reset scope to one Capacitor base for app and web, moved uploads to M2, re-dated the target, and took the change to the CEO.
  • Settled auth with security and the legal guardrails with legal (US first, no companion framing, escalation protocols, chat scoped to uploaded documents).
  • Set the support model with the Head of Program Management, won legal and support spend from the Head of Product under the new bets framework, kicked off the landing page, and dated the pilots: internal release, then a Queensland hospital, then soft launch.
  • Ran the whole app on the simulator before engineering started, which surfaced the expired-link and re-entry states.

Phase 5: Ask Heidi for Patients (June to November)

Built the safety case so patient-facing chat could follow the first release instead of blocking it.

  • Held chat out of the first release on purpose: a patient-facing hallucination removes the clinician from the loop.
  • Built the evidence base, the guardrail taxonomy, the must-fail and must-pass test corpus and the out-of-scope response design; worked the hallucination judge with the two Senior Clinical AI Engineers.
  • Set the jurisdiction order with regulatory (Australia, US, Canada, NZ; UK and EU deferred) and the rule that every answer cites the source.
  • Handed system prompt v1 and the ground truth test cases to engineering.

Research and advice

Six patient and clinician sessions. The GP backed the send flow and the M1 designs and asked for copy-link and a “what’s next” plan. The patient: trust runs through the clinician, SMS over email, transparency on data use. The chronic-condition patient sees six or more specialists, runs her family’s health through Dropbox, rated the app 5 out of 5 after a real appointment and asked for a correction layer. The co-founder of HotDoc gave me the frame for the open questions: clinician-introduced adoption is the compounding path, and without a per-patient revenue model the acquisition economics never close.

Stakeholder communication

I posted a Linear status update each week with an executive summary, updates, up next and blockers. Each fortnight I sent the CEO a short deck covering what was done, decided, blocked and still open, ending with the seven calls he needed to make and who owned each. The patient app entered the company’s new-bets framework, with a Thursday forum where resourcing scales with traction. The Growth PM went on leave before the Heidi 2 launch and the company moved me onto the commercial model, agents onboarding and community for Heidi 2. I paused patients for three weeks, told the CEO so in writing, and picked it back up the week he returned.

Impact

  • Clinicians who send a document to a patient retain about 25% better in their first week. The cohort is small and self-selecting, so I treat it as a signal rather than a result.
  • M0 reached 100% of free users in Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the US. Of the 7,500 or so clinicians who saw the send button in a month, 3% clicked it and under 1% sent, and that read is what moved the roadmap to Correspondence.
  • M1 is design complete with the architecture locked, the auth and compliance guardrails set, a support model agreed, legal and support spend approved and pilots dated.
  • The Ask Heidi for Patients guardrail system, test corpus and first system prompt are with engineering.

Reflection

This was the project that taught me to argue with my own dashboard. M0 worked for the clinicians who found it, and almost none of them found it. My first instinct was another placement test. The interviews said the real job was every document a clinician sends after a consult, so I changed the roadmap instead of the button, and Correspondence is the result.

If I ran it again I would ship the matched control events with the first release rather than the second, and I would start the legal document set in winter rather than let it sit behind the Heidi 2 launch, because it gated the pilot in the end. I would also push harder and earlier for a formal decision on patients as a segment. The approved spend was half an answer, and it left support planning late.

I carry forward the way the regulatory posture, the data model and the guardrail test set came together as one system rather than three documents. The product stays outside device classification because of decisions made in the first month, and that is the part I am proudest of.
